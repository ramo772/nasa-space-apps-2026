## Purpose

Capture the workshop's storytelling method for a future project pitch. These are communication
ideas and case examples, not team decisions, official competition rules, evidence of user
outcomes, or a choice of product format. Adapt them after the challenge, audience, and
evidence are known.

## Content

### The central aim

Help a judge **understand** one clear idea, **believe** it through evidence inside the
story, and **remember** one message. Start with the listener: an everyday user, a designer,
and a project evaluator may need different first explanations of the same facts. The
opening should answer the question that audience is likely to have.

### How the story moves

- The deck names **character, goal, conflict, stakes, action, result, and conclusion** as
  story elements. In a project pitch, make the user's goal and difficulty legible before
  listing features.
- Show both **chronology** (what happened next) and **cause and consequence** (why the next
  step followed). Check every transition: can the audience explain why one step leads to
  another?
- **And–But–Therefore** is an optional drafting tool: establish the situation, name the
  difficulty, then explain the response. Read the finished sentence naturally; the formula
  is a writing aid, not required wording.
- The presenter used a college project called *Cells* to model the move from a repeated
  file-search problem to a proposed navigation concept. The deck explicitly calls its
  interface diagram an educational reconstruction and the proposal **not a tested speed
  result**. Its figures and story are attributed to the presenter's recollection, not
  independently verified here.

### Opening and explanation choices

The workshop slides suggest starting with a familiar situation, introducing a difficulty
that raises a useful question, and letting the explanation answer that question. Bring in
a technical concept when the listener has a reason to need it. A familiar comparison can
help open a larger topic, but the comparison has limits and does not replace evidence.
Adapt these choices to the team's own facts, audience, and time; test what listeners
actually understand.

### Space Apps examples from the case lab

- **A Solar Tale:** The workshop highlights a first-person Sun narrator and chapter
  structure as ways to carry science through a story. NASA identifies the HerCode Space
  project as a [2025 Best Use of Storytelling winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/).
  The team's design question is what must remain scientifically accurate when an
  explanation becomes simpler.
- **TerraTales:** The workshop uses a Mother Earth and regional-character analogy to give
  climate information a shared context, then asks where the analogy stops and which
  scientific statements still need sources. NASA identifies [TerraTales as a 2024 Best
  Storytelling winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-international-space-apps-challenge-announces-2024-global-winners/).
  Its feature-list rewrite in the deck is a **teaching exercise**, not the original team's
  pitch or proof that users learned or changed behavior.
- **Henry's Farm Adventures / Zumorroda-X:** The workshop points to a farmer's journey and
  mini-game decisions as a way to make the next information useful. NASA describes
  [Zumorroda-X as a 2025 Art & Technology winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/).
  The team should ask what a player must know before each decision.

These cases illustrate communication choices. They do not establish which format our team
should build or whether a story-based design improves outcomes without testing.

### A practical drafting and rehearsal method

The workshop asks participants to leave with **one sentence** describing the project's
intended purpose, a **six-beat storyboard**, a **60–90-second opening** as a rehearsal
target, and **three intended takeaways**. The supplied text lists the following beats and
ends partway through beat 4:

1. **Hook and problem** — what needs explaining?
2. **User and stakes** — who needs this, and why?
3. **Design insight** — what connects the idea?
4. **Solution an…** — incomplete in the supplied text; full wording TBD.
5. **TBD** — not supplied.
6. **TBD** — not supplied.

## Open questions / uncertainty

- Obtain the remainder of the source text for beats 4–6 and any material after them.
- Obtain the deck, presenter, workshop date, and contributor attribution if available.
