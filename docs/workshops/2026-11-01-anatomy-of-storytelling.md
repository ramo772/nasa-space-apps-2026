## Content

### The central aim

The deck asks a storyteller to help a judge **understand** one clear idea, **believe** it through evidence inside the story, and **remember** one message. Start with the listener: an everyday user, a designer, and a project evaluator may need different first explanations of the same facts. The opening should answer the question that audience is likely to have.

### How the story moves

- The deck names **character, goal, conflict, stakes, action, result, and conclusion** as story elements. In a project pitch, make the user's goal and difficulty legible before listing features.
- Show both **chronology** (what happened next) and **cause and consequence** (why the next step followed). Check every transition: can the audience explain why one step leads to another?
- **And–But–Therefore** is offered as an optional drafting tool: establish the situation, name the difficulty, then explain the response. Read the finished sentence naturally; the formula is a writing aid, not required wording.
- The presenter uses a college project called *Cells* to model the move from a repeated file-search problem to a proposed navigation concept. The deck explicitly calls its interface diagram an educational reconstruction and the proposal **not a tested speed result**. Its figures and story are attributed to the presenter's recollection, not independently verified here.

### Opening and explanation choices

The workshop slides suggest starting with a familiar situation, introducing a difficulty that raises a useful question, and letting the explanation answer that question. Bring in a technical concept when the listener has a reason to need it. A familiar comparison can help open a larger topic, but the comparison has limits and does not replace evidence. Adapt these choices to the team's own facts, audience, and time; test what listeners actually understand.

### Space Apps examples from the case lab

- **A Solar Tale:** The workshop highlights a first-person Sun narrator and chapter structure as ways to carry science through a story. NASA identifies the HerCode Space project as a [2025 Best Use of Storytelling winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/). The team's design question is what must remain scientifically accurate when an explanation becomes simpler.
- **TerraTales:** The workshop uses a Mother Earth and regional-character analogy to give climate information a shared context, then asks where the analogy stops and which scientific statements still need sources. NASA identifies [TerraTales as a 2024 Best Storytelling winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-international-space-apps-challenge-announces-2024-global-winners/). Its feature-list rewrite in the deck is a **teaching exercise**, not the original team's pitch or proof that users learned or changed behavior.
- **Henry's Farm Adventures / Zumorroda-X:** The workshop points to a farmer's journey and mini-game decisions as a way to make the next information useful. NASA describes [Zumorroda-X as a 2025 Art & Technology winner](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/). The team should ask what a player must know before each decision.

These cases illustrate communication choices. They do not establish which format our team should build or whether a story-based design improves outcomes without testing.

### A practical drafting and rehearsal method

The workshop asks participants to leave with **one sentence** describing the project's intended purpose, a **six-beat storyboard**, a **60–90-second opening** as a rehearsal target, and **three intended takeaways**. The six beats are:

1. Hook and problem — what needs explaining?
2. User and stakes — who needs this, and why?
3. Design insight — what connects the idea?
4. Solution and demo — what can the viewer inspect?
5. Evidence and limits — what supports the claim, and what remains untested?
6. Intended impact and next test — what should change, and how would we check?

For each beat, draft a message, a visual, and its evidence or limit. The slide exercise allocates 30 minutes: frame the facts and audience (7), storyboard (9), rehearse (6), listen (5), and revise (3). Have a listener state the purpose they heard, what they recall, and what evidence is still missing. Revise one unclear connection and rehearse again.

### Match claims to evidence

Use wording that fits the project's stage: **proposal** for an idea, **prototype** for an interaction that can be shown, and **tested result** only when the test, participants, conditions, and observed outcome are available. Keep sources beside the claims they support. For charts, identify the source, measure, units, and period. A working demo may show a user path; it does not by itself prove learning, behavior change, speed, or broader impact.
